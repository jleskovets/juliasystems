We are all used to communication and interpersonal skills being classified as a soft skill for most IT professions — but for an analyst, communication becomes a hard skill: it is the primary working tool, not merely the ability to talk to people. And the very first skill worth developing when becoming an analyst is knowing how to ask the right questions.

We have already covered what kinds of requirements exist and what information they should contain. But where does that information come from in the first place? In most cases — from people. A business client describes their problem, a user explains their needs, a developer outlines technical constraints, a tester draws attention to possible scenarios, and the analyst's task is to bring all of this together into a coherent picture.

And here questions become one of the analyst's most important tools.

At first it seems like asking questions is very easy — but a good question is not simply a way to get information. It is a way to steer the conversation in the right direction and help the other person articulate something they may not even have thought about themselves.

Questions can broadly be divided into open and closed.

***Open questions***

An open question invites a detailed answer. It typically begins with words like "how", "why", "what", "tell me", "in what way".

For example:

"Walk me through how an order is currently placed" or "Why do you want to change this process?"

Such questions are especially useful at the beginning of a conversation, when the analyst does not yet know all the details. If you start immediately with closed questions, you can fairly quickly end up with a list of yes/no answers to standard template assumptions — and it will feel like you learned something, but you still have no real understanding of the process at all.

It is far more useful to start with an open question:

"Tell me how the approval of a request currently works, from the moment it is created to the point where a decision is made."

And then let the person talk. In the course of such a narrative, things often surface that you would never have thought to ask about.

***Closed questions***

A closed question invites a short and usually unambiguous answer: "yes", "no", a specific number, a date, or one of several given options.

These questions are especially useful once the analyst has built up a certain understanding and needs to verify a specific hypothesis or lock in an agreement.

In such cases a closed question works perfectly — there is no need to ask the person to walk through the whole process again; you simply need to check a specific assumption.

***So which questions should you ask?***

The short answer: both.

A good conversation with a client usually looks something like this:

Open question → receive information → follow-up closed questions → form a hypothesis → test it → ask another open question if a new area for investigation has emerged.

That is precisely why I would not try to teach an analyst a fixed set of questions to ask in a meeting. It is impossible to prepare a list in advance that will suit every project.

Far more important is learning to listen to the answers and ask the next question based on what the other person has just said.

One more important point: do not be afraid to ask obvious questions. Sometimes an analyst thinks "surely everyone understands this — it would be awkward to ask" — and then it turns out that every team member understood that "obvious" thing in a completely different way.

A good analyst does not try to demonstrate how much they already know. Their goal is to ensure that by the end of the conversation everyone genuinely has the same understanding of what was agreed. That is why every question that comes to mind is worth asking. And sometimes the most useful question of all is a very simple one: "What do you mean by that?"
