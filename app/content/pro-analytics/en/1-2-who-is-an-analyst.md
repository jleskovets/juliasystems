
So, let's figure out — who is an analyst?

At first glance, the answer seems like it should be very simple. But if you try to find a definition in professional standards or job descriptions, you'll quickly discover that no single answer exists.

Some say an analyst is a person who gathers requirements; others call them the link between business and development; still others believe an analyst designs solutions. Some are convinced that an analyst is a person who draws diagrams and writes documentation. All of these definitions are correct in their own way, but each of them describes only a small part of the profession.

The International Institute of Business Analysis (IIBA), the author of one of the most well-known global standards in the field — BABOK (Business Analysis Body of Knowledge) — defines an analyst not by job title, but by the work they perform. According to BABOK, anyone who performs analysis is an analyst, regardless of what their title says. Their core task is to understand the needs of stakeholders, analyze information, identify the real causes of problems, and help the organization choose solutions that create value. ([IIBA](https://www.iiba.org/globalassets/standards-and-resources/core-standard/iiba-core-standard.pdf?_t_ip=207.46.13.23&utm_source=chatgpt.com))

If you look carefully at this definition, you'll notice something interesting. It says almost nothing about documentation, diagrams, or specific tools. Tools change constantly. Analysts once drew diagrams on paper or a whiteboard; then came Visio, Enterprise Architect, draw.io, Miro, and dozens of other tools. The formats for requirements change, development methodologies change, modeling languages change, and even the roles within teams change — but the essence of the profession stays the same.

An analyst helps make sense of a situation when a ready-made answer does not yet exist. That is why I personally prefer to define an analyst not through a list of duties, but through their role in the team.

**An analyst is a person who reduces uncertainty — who helps turn the unknown into something clear and structured.**

They help answer the questions:

- What exactly are we trying to solve?
- Why did this problem arise in the first place?
- Who are we building the solution for?
- What constraints exist?
- How will we know the problem has actually been solved?

The less uncertainty remains, the easier it becomes for the entire team to work.

- Developers understand what needs to be implemented.
- Testers understand what needs to be verified.
- The business understands what value it will receive.
- Architects understand how the new solution will fit into the existing system.

That is precisely why a good analyst does not so much write requirements as help people reach a shared understanding of the problem and the future solution.

Later, once that understanding exists, the analyst creates documents, diagrams, specifications, and models. These are not the goal of the analyst's work — they are tools that help preserve and pass on the knowledge gained to other project participants.

In the following chapters we will look more closely at the fact that within the profession there are several distinct directions. Business analysts, systems analysts, product analysts, data analysts, and other specialists may use different tools and solve different problems. But they are all united by one common idea: they help people make better-informed decisions based on analysis rather than assumptions.
