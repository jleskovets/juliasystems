When we talk about business analysis, sooner or later we always arrive at business processes. It is genuinely hard to imagine an analyst's work without the need to understand exactly how something happens within a business.

**But what is a business process?**

If you look at various definitions, the wording differs slightly from source to source. In some places a business process is called a sequence of actions aimed at producing a specific result; in others, a set of interrelated actions that transform inputs into outputs. Some definitions separately emphasise the existence of a process customer, resources, participants, and an outcome.

Setting formalities aside, I define a business process quite simply:

A business process is a sequence of actions through which an organisation achieves a specific result.

Two words matter here: "actions" and "result".

For example, "selling a product" is more of a name for an activity. But if we start unpacking what happens inside, we get a process:

receive the order → check stock availability → take payment → pick the order → hand it to delivery → deliver to the customer.

A process has a beginning, some actions in the middle, and a result. It is not necessary for every action to be performed by a person — part of the process may be carried out automatically by information systems.

For example:

the customer places an order → the system checks availability → the payment system confirms payment → the warehouse receives a picking task.

And this is where business processes start to intersect with what we will discuss later in the section on systems.

**What kinds of business processes are there?**

There are quite a few classification schemes, and as is usually the case with classifications, different methodologies may use slightly different names.

If I simplify considerably, I would identify three broad groups.

*Core processes* — directly related to creating a product or service and delivering value to the customer.

For an online shop this might be selling and delivering a product; for a bank, opening an account, issuing a loan, or processing a payment.

*Support processes* — do not themselves create the core product for the customer, but enable the company to operate.

These include recruiting, procurement, accounting, IT infrastructure support, and many other processes.

*Management processes* — concerned with planning, controlling, and managing company activities: setting goals, budgeting, risk management, performance monitoring, and so on.

In practice the boundaries between these categories can be far from obvious. The same process may play completely different roles in different companies.

I actually think analysts should not get too attached to category names. What matters far more is understanding why a specific process exists and what result it is supposed to produce.

**What do business processes have in common?**

Strip away the subject domain and almost any business process can be examined through a few simple questions:

- What is the input to the process?
- What must happen for the process to start?
- Who participates in the process?
- Which people, departments, or systems perform the actions?
- What happens inside?
- What actions are carried out and in what order?
- How are decisions made?
- Are there conditions under which the process follows different scenarios?
- What is the result?
- How does the process end and what do we get as output?
- Who are we actually doing this for?

Because a process does not exist simply for the sake of a sequence of actions. In a healthy situation it should create some result or value for someone.

**And now the most interesting part — different domains**

At this point it often feels as though once an analyst has learned to describe business processes in one domain, everything else will be roughly the same. The answer is both yes and no. The general principle stays the same. In any domain there are participants, actions, inputs, outputs, rules, exceptions, and a result.

But the content of the process can be completely different.

In e-commerce we might work through:

search for a product → add to cart → place an order → pay → pick → deliver → return.

In fintech it might be:

create a payment → verify the customer → run security checks → authorise → process the payment → receive the result.

In investment business:

create an order → check instrument availability → run client and restriction checks → submit the instruction → execute → settle → book the transaction.

In the automotive sector the process might look quite different:

create a service booking → check time slot availability → confirm the booking → customer arrives → carry out the work → close the work order → payment.

But look closely and the structure is still familiar.

Somewhere a need or event arises. Someone or something triggers the process. Then a sequence of actions is carried out, decisions are made, systems exchange data, and at the end we get a specific result.

That is precisely why, when an analyst arrives in a completely new domain, they do not need to immediately know all the terminology and rules of that business.

What is far more important is learning to break an unfamiliar process down into its components and ask the right questions.

What triggers the process?

Who are its participants?

What happens next?

Why exactly that way?

What can go wrong?

What rules influence the process?

What should happen as a result?

Only after that can you start diving into what settlement means in investments, what a chargeback is in payments, or what a work order is in automotive service.

That is why I consider the ability to analyse business processes one of the most universal skills an analyst can have.

A subject domain can be learned. Terminology can be found in documentation. But the ability to look at an unfamiliar activity and understand how it is structured — that is a genuinely analytical skill.

And it is precisely this skill that we will build on as we move into BPMN and ways of visually describing business processes.
