If you have ever tried to write "perfect requirements", I have bad news for you: **perfect requirements do not exist.** And, moreover, requirements are never completely complete.

At first glance this sounds strange. Isn't the analyst's job precisely to gather all the necessary information and describe the future system in detail? Yes — but with an important qualifier: "necessary".

Requirements need to be detailed enough for the team to understand the task, agree on a solution, and implement it. But trying to describe absolutely everything in advance can produce the opposite result: the document becomes enormous, difficult to understand, and outdated before the developers have even finished reading it.

When every answer generates new questions, we can end up in a situation where the deeper we dive into detail, the more detail there is. You can try to anticipate everything, but at some point the cost of that analysis exceeds its value. And there are always deadlines, bugs, problems, and the simple human factor at every stage of the development process.

Furthermore, requirements do not exist in a vacuum — they depend on context, architecture, the existing system, legislation, team capabilities, and many other factors, and some decisions may only emerge during development itself. So at any given moment you are writing requirements "as well as you can right now", while always keeping in mind that something may have been missed and being aware of the risks.

That is why I would phrase the analyst's task slightly differently:

**Requirements should not be as detailed as possible, but as detailed as necessary for the specific task and the specific stage of work.**

Good requirements do not have to answer every possible question. But they should make it clear which questions still remain unanswered.

In my view, this is one of the important distinguishing qualities of a good analyst: they do not try to project complete certainty where none yet exists. They can spot gaps in knowledge, ask follow-up questions, and record agreements.

Because a requirement that is sufficient for discussing a business idea will be entirely insufficient for a developer. And a requirement that is sufficient to start development may need further clarification during implementation. That is perfectly normal.

**Good requirements are not ones in which no questions can be found. Good requirements are ones that allow the team to move forward, understanding what they are doing and why.**
